scRNA-seq Basics

NM

Nasir Mahmood Abbasi, PhD

Bioinformatics Educator

Tested on: R 4.3.2, Seurat v5.0.1, Ubuntu 24.04

Learning Objectives & Prerequisites

  • Prerequisites: Complete Linux, biological data formats, and basic statistics.
  • Objective: Process the 10x Genomics PBMC 3k dataset end-to-end. You'll learn to perform rigorous QC, normalize counts, select highly variable genes, reduce dimensionality (PCA/UMAP), and cluster cells.
  • Expected Output: A reproducible Seurat object, quantitative QC tables showing cell retention, a PCA Elbow Plot, and a final UMAP visualization of the 9 Leiden clusters.

From Raw Counts to Biological Insights

In this tutorial, we'll process the publicly available 10x Genomics PBMC 3k dataset (Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells from a healthy donor). The dataset contains 2,700 single cells sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500. This is the gold standard benchmarking dataset for single-cell analytics.

Dataset Provenance: 10x Genomics Datasets - PBMC 3k (v1 chemistry, hg19 reference)

Expert Insight: Pitfalls from Cancer Genomics

During my PhD research analyzing CTCLI quickly learned that generic tutorial thresholds can destroy your analysis. Malignant tumor cells are physically larger, transcriptionally active cells with higher gene and UMI counts, resulting in significantly higher total RNA counts than healthy T-cells.

If I had applied the standard "tutorial cutoff" of filtering cells with >2,500 genes (assuming them to be doublets), I would have literally deleted the malignant population I was trying to study. Always adjust your QC thresholds based on the expected biology of your specific tissue and disease state.

1. Loading the Data & Rigorous Quality Control (QC)

Quality control is arguably the most critical step in scRNA-seq analysis. We primarily look for three failure modes: empty droplets (ambient RNA), dying/apoptotic cells, and multiplets (two or more cells captured in one droplet). A high proportion of mitochondrial reads is a classic hallmark of a dying cell, as the cytoplasm leaks out but the mitochondria remain trapped in the membrane.

library(Seurat)

# Load the PBMC 3k 10x data matrix
pbmc.data <- Read10X(data.dir = "data/filtered_gene_bc_matrices/hg19/")
pbmc <- CreateSeuratObject(counts = pbmc.data, project = "pbmc3k", min.cells = 3, min.features = 200)

# Calculate the percentage of mitochondrial reads
pbmc[["percent.mt"]] <- PercentageFeatureSet(pbmc, pattern = "^MT-")

# Apply logical filtering based on visual inspection
pbmc <- subset(pbmc, subset = nFeature_RNA > 200 & nFeature_RNA < 2500 & percent.mt < 5)

Note: We successfully retained 97.7% of the cells, indicating a high-quality run. A retention rate below 80% usually indicates severe viability issues or a flawed dissociation protocol.

2. Normalization, PCA, and The Elbow Plot

After normalization and scaling, we run Principal Component Analysis (PCA). To determine how many Principal Components to use for downstream clustering, we generate an Elbow PlotWe look for the "elbow" where the variance explained drops off significantly.

pbmc <- NormalizeData(pbmc, normalization.method = "LogNormalize", scale.factor = 10000)
pbmc <- FindVariableFeatures(pbmc, selection.method = "vst", nfeatures = 2000)
pbmc <- ScaleData(pbmc, features = rownames(pbmc))
pbmc <- RunPCA(pbmc, features = VariableFeatures(object = pbmc))

3. Clustering and UMAP Visualization

Using the first 10 PCs, we construct a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) graph and apply the Louvain algorithm to partition the graph into clusters. Finally, we use UMAP to embed this high-dimensional graph into a 2D space for visualization.

pbmc <- FindNeighbors(pbmc, dims = 1:10)
pbmc <- FindClusters(pbmc, resolution = 0.5)
pbmc <- RunUMAP(pbmc, dims = 1:10)
DimPlot(pbmc, reduction = "umap", label = TRUE)

Conclusion

You've successfully processed a raw count matrix into a clustered, highly interpretable UMAP. The next steps involve mapping these numeric clusters to biological cell types using marker genes, and dealing with batch effects if you integrate multiple samples.

Full Reproducibility Script

Environment: R 4.3.2, Seurat v5.0.1, Seed: 42

library(Seurat)
set.seed(42)
pbmc.data <- Read10X(data.dir = "data/filtered_gene_bc_matrices/hg19/")
pbmc <- CreateSeuratObject(counts = pbmc.data, project = "pbmc3k", min.cells = 3, min.features = 200)
pbmc[["percent.mt"]] <- PercentageFeatureSet(pbmc, pattern = "^MT-")
pbmc <- subset(pbmc, subset = nFeature_RNA > 200 & nFeature_RNA < 2500 & percent.mt < 5)
pbmc <- NormalizeData(pbmc)
pbmc <- FindVariableFeatures(pbmc, selection.method = "vst", nfeatures = 2000)
pbmc <- ScaleData(pbmc)
pbmc <- RunPCA(pbmc)
pbmc <- FindNeighbors(pbmc, dims = 1:10)
pbmc <- FindClusters(pbmc, resolution = 0.5)
pbmc <- RunUMAP(pbmc, dims = 1:10)

Knowledge Check & Assessment

1. Troubleshooting

If a distinct UMAP cluster is driven almost entirely by mitochondrial gene expression, what does this indicate?

Reveal Answer

It strongly indicates that the cluster consists of dead or dying cells where the cytoplasm has ruptured, leaking cytoplasmic mRNA but retaining the larger mitochondria. This cluster is an artifact of the dissociation process and should be excluded by revisiting your QC thresholds.

Dr. Abbasi's Lab Notes & Practical Guidance

Field notes from single-cell & cancer genomics pipelines (BRIC, INSERM)

When I run our single-cell RNA-seq count matrix processing and normalization pipelines in our lab at the Bordeaux Institute of Oncology (BRIC INSERM), I always tell our students: don't rush your analysis, and don't skip your basic validation checks. You'll save yourself 40 hours of troubleshooting if you inspect your data distribution and quality metrics first. In my own research on Sézary syndrome across 7 patient-derived models, we've learned that you can't just assume default cutoffs like FDR < 0.05 or log2FC > 1.5 fit every single library. What I recommend to you is straightforward: test your distributions across at least 1,000 cells, check your outliers, and don't hesitate to tune your thresholds. We've seen that when you take time to look closely at your plots, you'll spot biological signals that you'd otherwise miss completely. Trust me, you'll be glad you didn't cut corners.

Here's what we do in our everyday workflows: we don't blindly follow generic pipelines. I've seen researchers discard valid signals because they didn't check their batch covariates or mitochondrial percentages. If you're struggling with your results, don't worry, we've faced identical roadblocks in our own projects. You can reach out to me directly or check our code on GitHub. We're here to help you get your project published. I'll always advise you to question your findings, verify what you're seeing, and make sure you're confident in your interpretations. We've built this workbench so you don't have to struggle alone. You'll get there, and we'll help you every step of the way. I want you to master these methods with us.
